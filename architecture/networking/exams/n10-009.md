## Domain 1.0: Networking Concepts (23% Weight · Questions 001–017)

### Q-N10-009-001 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 1 · single
Which Protocol Data Unit (PDU) is exchanged at the Transport layer (Layer 4) when using TCP?
- A) Frame
- B) Packet
- C) Segment
- D) Bit
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** In OSI and TCP/IP taxonomy, the Layer 4 PDU for connection-oriented TCP transmission is a **Segment** (or **Datagram** when using UDP).
- Why not A: A Frame is the Layer 2 (Data Link) PDU containing MAC headers and trailers.
- Why not B: A Packet is the Layer 3 (Network) PDU encapsulated with IP addressing.
- Why not D: A Bit is the physical electrical/optical signal transmitted across Layer 1.
- Ref: [modules/01-layers-and-packets.md](../modules/01-layers-and-packets.md#osi-7-layer-vs-tcpip-4-layer-model), RFC 9293
</details>

### Q-N10-009-002 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 1 · single
A network engineer inspects a raw Ethernet II packet trace and measures the total time required for a 1500-byte frame to traverse a 10 Gbps optical fiber spanning 1,000 kilometers ($c_{\text{fiber}} \approx 200,000\text{ km/s}$). What is the primary contributor to the total latency?
- A) Serialization (transmission) delay
- B) Propagation delay across the physical medium
- C) Queueing delay in the transceiver buffer
- D) Software interrupt processing overhead
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Propagation delay across 1,000 km of optical fiber is $t_{\text{prop}} = \frac{1,000\text{ km}}{200,000\text{ km/s}} = 5\text{ milliseconds}$ (5,000 microseconds). Serialization delay for 1500 bytes over a 10 Gbps link is $t_{\text{tx}} = \frac{1500 \times 8}{10 \times 10^9} = 1.2\text{ microseconds}$. Propagation delay exceeds transmission delay by more than three orders of magnitude ($5000\mu\text{s} \gg 1.2\mu\text{s}$).
- Why not A: At 10 Gbps, 1500 bytes serializes in merely 1.2 microseconds.
- Why not C, D: Transceiver buffering and kernel interrupts operate on sub-microsecond scales and do not dominate a 1,000 km WAN path.
- Ref: [modules/01-layers-and-packets.md](../modules/01-layers-and-packets.md#propagation-delay-vs-serialization-delay-math)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-003 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario
A transcontinental connection exhibits a Round-Trip Time (RTT) of 80 milliseconds across a 10 Gbps link. Assuming no packet loss, what is the minimum TCP receive window size ($W$) required to saturate the available bandwidth?
- A) 64 KB
- B) 800 KB
- C) 100 MB
- D) 1 GB
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP) dictates the amount of in-flight unacknowledged data necessary to keep the pipe saturated:
$$\text{BDP} = \text{Bandwidth} \times \text{RTT} = (10 \times 10^9\text{ bits/s}) \times 0.080\text{ s} = 800,000,000\text{ bits} = 100,000,000\text{ bytes} \approx 100\text{ MB}$$
Without a receive window of at least 100 MB (requiring RFC 7323 TCP Window Scale option), the sender will stall waiting for ACKs.
- Why not A: 64 KB is the legacy 16-bit window ceiling without window scaling, which caps throughput on an 80ms RTT link to $\frac{64\text{ KB} \times 8}{0.080\text{ s}} \approx 6.55\text{ Mbps}$.
- Why not B: 800 KB only yields 80 Mbps throughput.
- Why not D: 1 GB is ten times larger than the required BDP.
- Ref: [modules/01-layers-and-packets.md](../modules/01-layers-and-packets.md#bandwidth-delay-product-bdp-calculation), RFC 7323
</details>

### Q-N10-009-004 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · multi
Which of the following statements correctly describe Ethernet II frame fields and link-layer mechanics? (Select TWO)
- A) The minimum valid untagged Ethernet II frame size on the wire is 64 bytes (including header and 4-byte FCS, excluding preamble/SFD).
- B) The EtherType field for standard IPv4 traffic is `0x86DD`.
- C) An 802.1Q VLAN tag injects 4 bytes immediately after the source MAC address, shifting EtherType to `0x8100`.
- D) Ethernet switches forward multicast frames only to ports configured as trunk ports.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A and C.**
- A is correct: Standard Ethernet requires a minimum payload of 46 bytes, plus 14 bytes header (Dst MAC + Src MAC + EtherType) and 4 bytes CRC/FCS = 64 bytes. Payloads under 46 bytes are padded.
- C is correct: IEEE 802.1Q inserts a 4-byte tag (`0x8100` TPID + 16-bit TCI containing 3-bit PCP, 1-bit DEI, and 12-bit VLAN ID) before the payload EtherType.
- Why not B: `0x86DD` is IPv6; standard IPv4 is `0x0800`.
- Why not D: Unregistered multicast is flooded to all ports in the broadcast domain unless IGMP snooping is active.
- Ref: [modules/02-link-layer-and-switching.md](../modules/02-link-layer-and-switching.md#ethernet-ii-frame-format), [02-arp-and-ethernet.test.ts](../examples/02-arp-and-ethernet.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-005 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · hands-on
An enterprise network allocates the prefix `192.168.10.0/24`. The administrator needs to partition this block into four equal subnets for separate engineering teams. What is the subnet mask in dotted-decimal format, and how many usable host addresses exist per subnet?
- A) `255.255.255.192` with 62 usable hosts
- B) `255.255.255.224` with 30 usable hosts
- C) `255.255.255.240` with 14 usable hosts
- D) `255.255.255.128` with 126 usable hosts
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Partitioning a `/24` into 4 equal subnets requires borrowing 2 host bits ($2^2 = 4$), resulting in a `/26` prefix ($24 + 2 = 26$).
A `/26` mask has 26 leading ones: `11111111.11111111.11111111.11000000`, which converts to `255.255.255.192`.
Each `/26` subnet has $2^{32 - 26} = 2^6 = 64$ total IP addresses. Subtracting the network identifier and the broadcast address leaves $64 - 2 = 62$ usable host addresses.
- Why not B: `255.255.255.224` is `/27`, which yields 8 subnets with 30 hosts each.
- Why not C: `255.255.255.240` is `/28` (16 subnets with 14 hosts).
- Why not D: `255.255.255.128` is `/25` (2 subnets with 126 hosts).
- Ref: [modules/03-ip-addressing-and-routing.md](../modules/03-ip-addressing-and-routing.md#cidr-math--vlsm), [03-cidr-and-routing-trie.test.ts](../examples/03-cidr-and-routing-trie.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-006 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · single
In IPv6 architecture, what is the scope and prefix of a Link-Local unicast address?
- A) `fc00::/7`
- B) `fe80::/10`
- C) `2001::/16`
- D) `ff02::/16`
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** IPv6 Link-Local unicast addresses are assigned from the `fe80::/10` prefix (specifically `fe80::/64` in practical host configuration). They are valid only within the local link/broadcast domain and routers must never forward them across link boundaries.
- Why not A: `fc00::/7` is Unique Local Address (ULA) space (equivalent to IPv4 RFC 1918 private space).
- Why not C: `2000::/3` (including `2001::`) is Global Unicast Address (GUA) space.
- Why not D: `ff00::/8` (e.g. `ff02::`) designates multicast addresses.
- Ref: [modules/03-ip-addressing-and-routing.md](../modules/03-ip-addressing-and-routing.md#ipv6-wire-header-and-mechanics), RFC 4291
</details>

### Q-N10-009-007 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario
A dual-stack client attempts to establish a TCP connection to `api.example.com`. The DNS resolver returns both an IPv4 A record and an IPv6 AAAA record. According to RFC 8305 (Happy Eyeballs v2), how does the client initiate the connection?
- A) It attempts IPv4 first and falls back to IPv6 only after a 30-second TCP connection timeout.
- B) It sends SYN packets over both IPv4 and IPv6 simultaneously at $t = 0$.
- C) It prefers IPv6, emits an IPv6 SYN immediately, and schedules an IPv4 SYN after a Connection Attempt Delay of ~250–300 ms if IPv6 has not completed its handshake.
- D) It queries the local ARP table to determine which protocol has lower ping latency.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** RFC 8305 specifies that clients initiate connection attempts by favoring IPv6. To avoid degrading user experience when IPv6 paths are broken or blackholed, the client starts the IPv6 connection at $t = 0$ and starts a timer (recommended 250 ms, "Resolution Delay" / "Connection Attempt Delay"). If IPv6 SYN-ACK is not received within that window, the IPv4 connection attempt is initiated concurrently, and whichever handshake completes first is retained while the slower socket is closed.
- Why not A: Traditional serial fallback caused multi-second user hangs.
- Why not B: Simultaneous dual SYN at $t = 0$ generates unnecessary load and connection churn across carrier networks.
- Why not D: ARP operates only on IPv4 and does not measure end-to-end transport latency.
- Ref: [modules/03-ip-addressing-and-routing.md](../modules/03-ip-addressing-and-routing.md#happy-eyeballs-v2-rfc-8305), RFC 8305
</details>

### Q-N10-009-008 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 1 · single
Which field in the UDP header ensures end-to-end data integrity over IPv4, and what is its status in IPv6?
- A) Sequence Number; mandatory in IPv4, deprecated in IPv6.
- B) Checksum; optional in IPv4 (0 denotes uncalculated), mandatory in IPv6.
- C) Urgent Pointer; mandatory in IPv4, optional in IPv6.
- D) Window Size; optional in both IPv4 and IPv6.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The UDP checksum covers the UDP header, payload, and the IP pseudo-header. In IPv4, calculating the checksum is optional (a transmitted value of `0x0000` indicates no checksum was generated). In IPv6, because the IPv6 base header dropped the header checksum field, UDP checksum calculation is strictly mandatory (RFC 8200).
- Why not A, C, D: UDP headers are fixed at 8 bytes and contain only Source Port, Destination Port, Length, and Checksum. Sequence numbers, urgent pointers, and window sizes exist only in TCP.
- Ref: [modules/05-udp-and-sockets.md](../modules/05-udp-and-sockets.md#udp-header-wire-format-rfc-768), RFC 8200
</details>

### Q-N10-009-009 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario
An engineer calls `connect()` on a UDP socket in Linux. What kernel state change occurs?
- A) A 3-way handshake is initiated with the remote host.
- B) The socket transitions to `ESTABLISHED`, filters incoming datagrams to only match the specified remote IP and port, and enables the use of `send()` and `recv()` without destination addresses.
- C) The kernel changes the underlying protocol to TCP.
- D) The kernel guarantees reliable in-order packet delivery for subsequent UDP transmissions.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Calling `connect()` on a `SOCK_DGRAM` UDP socket does not transmit any packets over the wire. Instead, it binds the socket descriptor to a foreign 2-tuple (remote IP, remote port) in kernel memory. This allows the application to call `send()`/`write()` without passing `sockaddr_in`, restricts incoming datagrams delivered to that socket to only those originating from that foreign address, and enables asynchronous asynchronous ICMP error delivery (`ECONNREFUSED`).
- Why not A: UDP is stateless and connectionless; no handshake packets are sent.
- Why not C: The socket family and protocol remain `AF_INET` / `IPPROTO_UDP`.
- Why not D: `connect()` does not add ACK, retransmission, or flow control logic to UDP.
- Ref: [modules/05-udp-and-sockets.md](../modules/05-udp-and-sockets.md#the-connected-udp-socket), [05-udp-and-socket-lifecycle.test.ts](../examples/05-udp-and-socket-lifecycle.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-010 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · single
What is the primary role of the `TIME_WAIT` state in the TCP state machine (RFC 9293)?
- A) To hold the socket open while background DNS queries resolve.
- B) To ensure the final ACK sent by the active closer reaches the remote peer and to allow lingering delayed segments from the previous connection incarnation to expire from the network ($2 \times \text{MSL}$).
- C) To permit the server to send bulk unacknowledged data before terminating.
- D) To negotiate TLS 1.3 cryptographic session tickets before socket release.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The active closer (the endpoint that issues the first FIN) enters `TIME_WAIT` upon receiving the remote peer's FIN and transmitting its final ACK. It remains in `TIME_WAIT` for $2 \times \text{MSL}$ (Maximum Segment Lifetime, typically 60 seconds in Linux) for two reasons: (1) if its final ACK was lost, the remote peer will retransmit FIN, which can be re-acknowledged; (2) it ensures all delayed duplicate segments traversing internet queues dissipate before the same 4-tuple can be reused.
- Why not A: DNS resolution precedes connection initiation, not termination.
- Why not C: Data cannot be sent in `TIME_WAIT`.
- Why not D: TLS tickets are negotiated during or immediately after the TLS handshake, long before connection closure.
- Ref: [modules/06-tcp-internals.md](../modules/06-tcp-internals.md#the-11-states-of-rfc-9293), [06-tcp-state-machine-and-nagle.test.ts](../examples/06-tcp-state-machine-and-nagle.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-011 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 3 · scenario
A web service demonstrates persistent 40 ms latency spikes when transmitting small API responses (e.g., 200-byte JSON payloads). Analysis reveals that the application makes two consecutive `write()` system calls (first writing HTTP headers, then writing the JSON body) without setting `TCP_NODELAY`. What pathological interaction causes this delay?
- A) Epoll edge-triggered starvation.
- B) Conflict between Nagle's algorithm on the sender and Delayed ACKs on the receiver.
- C) TCP SYN Cookie recalculation overhead.
- D) Path MTU Discovery ICMP black hole.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Nagle's algorithm inhibits the sender from transmitting small segments (less than MSS) if any previously transmitted data remains unacknowledged. The first `write()` transmits headers as a small segment. When the second `write()` occurs, the headers segment is unacknowledged, so Nagle buffers the payload. Meanwhile, the receiver implements Delayed ACKs (RFC 1122), waiting up to 40 ms (or 200 ms) in hopes of piggybacking an ACK onto outgoing data. The two algorithms deadlock until the receiver's Delayed ACK timer expires. Enabling `TCP_NODELAY` disables Nagle and eliminates the 40 ms stall.
- Why not A: Epoll edge-triggering does not delay TCP packet transmission on existing open sockets.
- Why not C: SYN cookies operate only during connection establishment under SYN flood conditions.
- Why not D: PMTUD black holes drop packets larger than MTU; small 200-byte packets are unaffected.
- Ref: [modules/06-tcp-internals.md](../modules/06-tcp-internals.md#nagles-algorithm-vs-delayed-acks-the-40ms-deadlock), [06-tcp-state-machine-and-nagle.test.ts](../examples/06-tcp-state-machine-and-nagle.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-012 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · single
In classic TCP congestion control (RFC 5681), how does the Additive Increase / Multiplicative Decrease (AIMD) algorithm adjust the congestion window ($W$)?
- A) Increments $W$ by $1\text{ MSS}$ per RTT during congestion avoidance; reduces $W$ to $1\text{ MSS}$ on any timeout or dupACK.
- B) Increments $W$ by $1\text{ MSS}$ per RTT during congestion avoidance; cuts $W$ by half ($W \leftarrow W / 2$) upon receiving 3 duplicate ACKs.
- C) Doubles $W$ every RTT until maximum buffer capacity is exhausted.
- D) Multiplies $W$ by 2 on success and decrements $W$ by 1 on loss.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** AIMD increases the congestion window linearly by $1\text{ MSS}$ for every RTT of successful delivery ($W \leftarrow W + 1$) during Congestion Avoidance, and reduces the window multiplicatively by half ($W \leftarrow W \times 0.5$) upon detecting packet loss via Fast Retransmit (3 duplicate ACKs). This mathematical property guarantees convergence to fair and Pareto-optimal bandwidth allocation.
- Why not A: Resetting $W$ to $1\text{ MSS}$ occurs only on retransmission timeout (RTO), not on fast retransmit.
- Why not C: Doubling $W$ every RTT is Slow Start, not AIMD.
- Why not D: Multiplicative increase / additive decrease causes divergence and severe network congestion collapse.
- Ref: [modules/07-congestion-control.md](../modules/07-congestion-control.md#formal-foundation-aimd-and-chiu-jain-convergence), [07-congestion-control-sim.test.ts](../examples/07-congestion-control-sim.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-013 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · single
What fundamental paradigm shift distinguishes BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and RTT) from loss-based congestion control algorithms such as Reno and CUBIC?
- A) BBR treats packet loss as the sole indicator of network congestion.
- B) BBR models the physical network by independently measuring maximum bottleneck delivery rate ($\text{BtlBw}$) and minimum propagation delay ($\text{RTprop}$), maintaining queue occupancy near zero without requiring buffer loss.
- C) BBR requires router hardware modification with Active Queue Management (CoDel).
- D) BBR disables TCP window scaling to prevent bufferbloat.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Classic congestion control algorithms (Reno, CUBIC) are loss-based: they inflate the congestion window until a bottleneck buffer overflows and drops a packet. BBR treats packet loss and buffer queueing as distinct phenomena; it explicitly builds an internal model of the path by tracking $\text{BtlBw}$ and $\text{RTprop}$, sizing its in-flight data to match Kleinrock's optimal operating point ($W = \text{BtlBw} \times \text{RTprop}$), thereby maximizing throughput while minimizing latency and bufferbloat.
- Why not A: Loss-based control is CUBIC's design; BBR deliberately avoids treating packet loss as an immediate capacity ceiling.
- Why not C: BBR is entirely sender-side; it requires no router assistance or AQM.
- Why not D: Sizing in-flight data to high BDP paths requires window scaling.
- Ref: [modules/07-congestion-control.md](../modules/07-congestion-control.md#bbr-bottleneck-bandwidth-and-rtt-model-based-control), [07-congestion-control-sim.test.ts](../examples/07-congestion-control-sim.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-014 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 1 · single
Which bit combination in the IP header DiffServ / ECN field indicates that an intermediate router experienced incipient congestion and marked the packet rather than dropping it?
- A) `ECT(0)` (`0b10`)
- B) `ECT(1)` (`0b01`)
- C) `CE` (Congestion Experienced, `0b11`)
- D) `Not-ECT` (`0b00`)
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** RFC 3168 reserves the lowest 2 bits of the IPv4 TOS / IPv6 Traffic Class byte for Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN). Endpoints negotiate ECN by sending `ECT(0)` (`0b10`) or `ECT(1)` (`0b01`). When an ECN-capable router detects queue growth exceeding AQM thresholds, it rewrites these two bits to `CE` (`Congestion Experienced`, `0b11`). The receiver reads `CE` and reflects it back to the sender via the `ECE` flag in the TCP header.
- Why not A, B: `ECT(0)` and `ECT(1)` signify that the sending endpoint supports ECN.
- Why not D: `Not-ECT` (`0b00`) indicates the packet does not participate in ECN; routers must drop it upon congestion.
- Ref: [modules/07-congestion-control.md](../modules/07-congestion-control.md#ecn-explicit-congestion-notification-rfc-3168-and-l4s-rfc-9330)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-015 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario
An administrator captures network packets using Wireshark and notes that DNS responses over UDP are truncated, with the `TC` bit set. What standard protocol mechanism allows the client to advertise a buffer larger than 512 bytes to receive full DNS responses without falling back to TCP?
- A) DNSSEC RRSIG
- B) Extension Mechanisms for DNS (EDNS0, RFC 6891) via an OPT pseudo-record
- C) DNS over HTTPS (DoH)
- D) DNS Name Compression pointers
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Standard RFC 1035 UDP DNS datagrams are restricted to 512 bytes. RFC 6891 introduced EDNS0, which allows a client to include an `OPT` pseudo-resource record in the Additional section of the query advertising its UDP payload buffer size (e.g. 1232 or 4096 bytes). This permits large answers (such as DNSSEC cryptographic keys) to return over UDP without triggering the `TC` (Truncation) bit and forcing a fallback to TCP port 53.
- Why not A: RRSIG contains the digital signature; it actually increases packet size.
- Why not C: DoH transmits DNS queries over HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 via TLS, not raw UDP port 53.
- Why not D: Name compression optimizes domain label pointers (`0xC000`) but cannot expand the 512-byte payload ceiling.
- Ref: [modules/08-dns.md](../modules/08-dns.md#edns0-and-buffer-sizing-rfc-6891), [08-dns-wire-format.test.ts](../examples/08-dns-wire-format.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-016 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 3 · scenario
A Node.js backend cluster experiences severe latency spikes in its HTTP client requests whenever it performs high-volume DNS lookups for external APIs, despite low CPU and memory utilization. Profiling shows worker threads stuck in `uv_getaddrinfo`. What architectural issue causes this?
- A) The V8 garbage collector is halting network sockets.
- B) Node.js `dns.lookup()` executes synchronously on the libuv threadpool (`UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE`, default 4 threads), blocking file I/O and crypto operations during slow DNS resolution.
- C) Node.js does not support IPv6 AAAA records.
- D) TCP port exhaustion on loopback socket pairs.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Node.js `dns.lookup()` delegates to the operating system's POSIX `getaddrinfo(3)` C library function, which is synchronous and blocking. To prevent blocking the main event loop, libuv offloads `getaddrinfo` calls to its internal threadpool, which defaults to only 4 worker threads. When hundreds of concurrent outgoing requests trigger DNS lookups, the 4 threads become saturated, queueing all subsequent DNS queries, file system operations, and cryptographic operations. Replacing `dns.lookup` with `dns.resolve*()` (which uses c-ares for asynchronous non-blocking UDP queries) or increasing `UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE` resolves the bottleneck.
- Why not A: V8 GC pauses affect JavaScript execution, not background libuv thread scheduling.
- Why not C: `getaddrinfo` natively queries both A and AAAA records.
- Why not D: DNS lookups over UDP do not consume TCP loopback ports.
- Ref: [modules/08-dns.md](../modules/08-dns.md#production-pitfall-node-threadpool-starvation)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-017 · Domain 1.0 · difficulty 2 · multi
Which cryptographic enhancements were introduced in TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) compared to TLS 1.2? (Select TWO)
- A) Removal of static RSA key transport and non-PFS Diffie-Hellman in favor of mandatory Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (ECDHE / DHE).
- B) Addition of RC4 and 3DES stream ciphers for legacy IoT devices.
- C) Reduction of the standard handshake from 2 RTTs to a single round-trip (1-RTT) by transmitting key share parameters in the initial ClientHello.
- D) Inclusion of MD5 and SHA-1 hashing in certificate signature chains.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A and C.**
- A is correct: TLS 1.3 eliminates static RSA key exchange (which lacked forward secrecy; if the private server key was compromised, historical recorded traffic could be decrypted) and mandates Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman key exchanges (X25519, secp256r1) guaranteeing Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS).
- C is correct: In TLS 1.2, the client and server required 2 full round-trips to negotiate cipher suites and exchange keys before transmitting application data. In TLS 1.3, the client speculatively provides `key_share` extensions in its `ClientHello`, allowing the server to respond with `ServerHello` and immediately transmit encrypted `Finished` and application data in 1-RTT.
- Why not B, D: TLS 1.3 completely purged legacy insecure primitives, including RC4, 3DES, MD5, SHA-1, CBC-mode ciphers, and arbitrary renegotiation.
- Ref: [modules/09-tls-1-3-and-pki-on-the-wire.md](../modules/09-tls-1-3-and-pki-on-the-wire.md#the-1-rtt-handshake-anatomy), [09-tls-wire-dissection.test.ts](../examples/09-tls-wire-dissection.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-018 · Domain 2.0 · difficulty 1 · single
How does an Ethernet switch learn the association between MAC addresses and its physical ports?
- A) It inspects the Destination MAC address of incoming frames.
- B) It inspects the Source MAC address of incoming frames and records it in its Content-Addressable Memory (CAM) table along with the ingress port.
- C) It issues periodic SNMP broadcast queries to all connected hosts.
- D) It queries the default gateway via ARP.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** A layer 2 switch operates via dynamic MAC learning: when a frame ingresses on a port, the switch reads the **Source MAC** address and adds or refreshes an entry in its CAM table mapping that MAC address to the arrival port and VLAN ID. Destination MAC is used only for the forwarding lookup (or flooding if unknown).
- Why not A: Destination MAC indicates where the packet should go; looking at destination MAC would not reveal which device is attached to the ingress port.
- Why not C, D: Switches learn topology passively from data plane frame headers, not active SNMP or ARP polling.
- Ref: [modules/02-link-layer-and-switching.md](../modules/02-link-layer-and-switching.md#switching-and-the-cam-table), [02-arp-and-ethernet.test.ts](../examples/02-arp-and-ethernet.test.ts)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-019 · Domain 2.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario
An attacker connects a malicious workstation to a switch port and generates 500,000 frames per second, each with a randomized fake source MAC address. What condition does this cause on an unhardened switch?
- A) BGP route flapping.
- B) CAM table overflow, forcing the switch to flood subsequent unicast traffic to all ports like a hub (Fail-Open behavior).
- C) STP Root Bridge takeover.
- D) Immediate physical port burnout.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Switches have finite CAM table storage (e.g. 8K to 128K entries). In a MAC flooding attack, randomized source MACs fill the table to capacity. Once the CAM table is saturated, legitimate host entries are evicted. When the switch receives unicast frames destined for legitimate hosts, the destination MAC is not found in the CAM table, forcing the switch to flood the frame out of every port in that VLAN ("Fail-Open"), allowing the attacker's sniffing tool to capture sensitive traffic. Configuring Port Security (limiting MACs per port) mitigates this attack.
- Why not A: BGP is a Layer 3 routing protocol between autonomous systems.
- Why not C: STP root takeover requires crafting Spanning Tree BPDU packets with lower bridge priority.
- Why not D: High packet rates consume CPU and memory buffers but do not cause physical hardware burnout.
- Ref: [modules/02-link-layer-and-switching.md](../modules/02-link-layer-and-switching.md#cam-table-overflow-and-port-security)
</details>

### Q-N10-009-020 · Domain 2.0 · difficulty 2 · ordering
Order the sequence of packets exchanged when Host A (`192.168.1.10`) sends an IP packet to Host B (`192.168.1.20`) on the same local subnet for the first time, starting from an empty ARP cache:
1. Host A emits ARP Request (Broadcast: `ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff`).
2. Host B inspects ARP Request, learns Host A's IP-to-MAC mapping, and emits ARP Reply (Unicast to Host A).
3. Host A receives ARP Reply, updates its local ARP table, and transmits the encapsulated IP packet.
4. Host A checks its local ARP cache and identifies a cache miss for `192.168.1.20`.
- A) 4 $\rightarrow$ 1 $\rightarrow$ 2 $\rightarrow$ 3

### Q-N10-009-059 · Domain 5.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario

A packet capture reveals outgoing TCP data packets with the Don't Fragment (DF) bit set, but the client never receives data larger than 1400 bytes. What network anomaly is occurring?
- A) Path MTU Discovery (PMTUD) Black Hole.
- B) DNS root zone corruption.
- C) CAM table exhaustion.
- D) TCP TIME_WAIT port exhaustion.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** PMTUD (RFC 1191) sets the Don't Fragment (DF) flag in the IPv4 header. - Why not D: TIME_WAIT exhaustion prevents establishing new connections; it does not drop mid-stream large packets.

### Q-N10-009-062 · Domain 5.0 · difficulty 2 · scenario

- B) Hop 4 is rate-limiting ICMP generation or offloading ICMP processing to its slow control-plane CPU; because subsequent hops show 0% loss, there is no true packet loss in the forwarding data plane.
- C) The destination server is dropping TCP SYN packets.
